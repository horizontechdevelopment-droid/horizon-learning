# Guide 25: Safe Network Discovery

**Estimated Time:** 35–45 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner

---

## Have You Ever Wondered...

...how cybersecurity professionals know where the line is between learning and unauthorized activity?

Many tools used by defenders can also be misused.

The difference is not the tool.

The difference is **permission**.

In this guide, you'll learn how to practice safely, ethically, and responsibly.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this guide, you should be able to:

- Explain why authorization matters.
- Recognize safe environments for learning.
- Understand the importance of ethical behavior.
- Know when it is appropriate to use cybersecurity tools.

---

## Goal

Develop good habits that will guide your cybersecurity journey.

---

## Why This Matters

Cybersecurity is built on trust.

Organizations trust professionals to help protect their systems.

That trust depends on using knowledge responsibly.

Building ethical habits now will serve you throughout your career.

---

## Plain-English Explanation

Imagine owning a flashlight.

Using it to inspect your own attic is perfectly reasonable.

Using it to look through someone else's windows without permission is not.

The flashlight didn't change.

The situation did.

Cybersecurity tools are no different.

---

## New Terms

- Authorization
- Permission
- Scope
- Assessment

---

# Permission Comes First

Before using any cybersecurity tool, ask yourself:

- Do I own this system?
- Do I have explicit permission?
- Am I working in an approved training environment?

If the answer is "no" or "I'm not sure," stop and ask first.

---

# Safe Places to Learn

Excellent environments include:

- Your own computer
- Your own home network
- Virtual machines
- Practice labs
- Capture-the-Flag environments
- Training platforms that invite testing

These environments are designed for learning.

---

# Places You Should Not Test

Do **not** assume permission simply because a system is publicly accessible.

Examples include:

- Someone else's home network
- School networks
- Workplace systems (unless authorized)
- Public Wi-Fi networks
- Websites you don't own
- Internet-connected devices that aren't yours

Visibility is **not** permission.

---

# Responsible Curiosity

Curiosity is one of the most valuable qualities in cybersecurity.

Ask questions.

Experiment.

Learn.

But always do so in environments where your actions are expected and authorized.

Responsible curiosity builds trust.

---

!!! tip "Learning Tip"

    Whenever you're unsure whether something is appropriate to test, pause and ask.

    Seeking clarification is a sign of professionalism, not weakness.

---

!!! info "Looking Under the Hood"

    Many organizations clearly define what systems may be tested, what techniques are allowed, and when testing may occur.

    This is often called the **scope** of an assessment.

---

!!! info "Why Professionals Do This"

    Professional security assessments begin with written authorization.

    This protects both the organization and the security professionals performing the work.

---

## Try It Yourself

Think about your current environment.

Create two lists.

### Safe to Practice

Examples might include:

- Your laptop
- Your desktop
- Your virtual machine
- Your home lab
- Your personal router

### Not Safe to Practice

Examples might include:

- Your employer's network
- School systems
- Public Wi-Fi
- A friend's computer (without permission)

If you're ever unsure, ask before testing.

---

## Reflection Questions

Without looking back:

1. Why is permission important?
2. What does authorization mean?
3. Can a publicly accessible website be tested without permission?
4. What is a safe practice environment?
5. Why is responsible behavior important in cybersecurity?

---

!!! success "Memory Helper"

    Own it

    or

    Have permission.

    If neither is true...

    Don't test it.

---

!!! warning "Common Beginner Mistake"

    Some beginners assume that if a system responds to network traffic, it's acceptable to scan.

    It isn't.

    Authorization—not technical possibility—determines whether testing is appropriate.

---

!!! example "Real-World Example"

    Imagine borrowing a friend's car.

    Even if you know how to drive it, you still ask before taking it for a drive.

    Cybersecurity follows the same principle.

    Ability never replaces permission.

---
